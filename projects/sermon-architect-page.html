Step 1 · Exegetical Foundation

Passage & Exegesis

Expository preaching begins and ends with the text. The sermon's structure, points, and thrust must emerge from the passage — not be imposed upon it. Do your exegetical work here before building anything else.

Lloyd Perry — Expository Textual Integrity In expository preaching, the text is not merely a launching pad — it is the sermon's architecture. Perry insists: the main points arise from the passage's own divisions; every assertion must be traceable to a specific verse; the preacher's voice serves the text's voice. Begin by mapping the passage's structure before you write a single outline point.
Textual Analysis
Perry — Textual Analysis as the Exegetical Engine Faithful exposition requires moving through several layers of the text before any homiletical decisions are made. Historical-grammatical analysis, word study, syntactical observation, and intertextual tracing are not optional scholarly extras — they are the work of finding what the text actually says, means, and demands. Each layer informs and constrains the next.

Step 2 · Bryan Chapell

Fallen Condition Focus

Every passage addresses a human fallenness that Christ redeems. Identifying the FCF prevents moralism and keeps Christ at the center.

Bryan Chapell — Christ-Centered Preaching Chapell argues that a sermon without a clear FCF lapses into mere advice-giving. The FCF is the universal human condition — a consequence of the Fall — that the text addresses and that the Gospel answers. It must be present in the text, real in the audience, and resolved in Christ.

Step 3 · John Peckham

Relational Doxology

Peckham's framework situates all of redemption within God's relational pursuit of his people — and the ultimate aim is the overflow of praise.

John Peckham — Canonical Theology & Relational Doxology For Peckham, God's love is not merely volitional or ontological — it is genuinely relational and affective. Scripture narrates a cosmic love story: God pursuing, the creature resisting or responding, and the whole creation moving toward doxological consummation. Preaching should trace this arc: God's relational initiative → human response → doxological overflow.

Step 4 · Perry §1

The Introduction

Perry identifies five functions of a good introduction: capture attention, surface the need, introduce the text, state the proposition, and preview the body.

Perry — Introduction Principles The introduction should create a "felt need" that the sermon will address. It must be brief (no more than 15% of sermon time), earn the right to be heard, and bridge the world of the text with the world of the congregation. Perry warns against "cold openings" that have no connection to the sermon's central thrust.

Step 5 · Perry §2

Proposition, Interrogative Adverb & Propositional Transition

In expository preaching, the proposition is the exegetical idea re-voiced for your congregation today. From it flows an interrogative adverb (Why? How? What?) that raises the question the body will answer — and a propositional transition (the keyword plural noun) that signals the structure of the main points.

Perry — The Expository Proposition The expository proposition differs from the exegetical idea in one key way: it is addressed to your congregation, in present tense, as a living word. It must be: (1) a complete declarative sentence, (2) in present tense, (3) directly derived from the passage's own central assertion, (4) comprehensive enough to contain all main points, and (5) simple enough to repeat from memory. Perry: "If you can't say it in one sentence, you don't yet know what you're preaching."
Interrogative Adverb

This is the single question — built around an interrogative adverb (Why? How? What? When? Where?) — that your proposition raises and your main points collectively answer. It acts as the hinge between the proposition and the body. The keyword you select below must appear naturally in this question.

Propositional Transition

The Propositional Transition is a plural noun embedded in the interrogative question above that previews the structure of the body. It signals to the congregation that multiple parallel points are coming, all of the same grammatical and logical kind. Choose the keyword that best describes what your main points are — then verify it fits your interrogative question naturally.

Step 6 · Perry §3–5

Sermon Body — Text-Derived Points

In expository preaching, main points are not invented — they are discovered. Each point must be traceable to a specific textual division of the pericope you mapped in Step 1.

Perry — Expository Body Development Each main point must: (1) arise from a natural division of the passage, (2) be supported by the specific verse(s) in that division, (3) restate the passage's assertion in homiletical form, (4) include exposition of the text, an illustration, and application. Points must be parallel in grammatical form and progressive in redemptive development. Perry warns: a point without a verse is an opinion, not exposition.
Step 7 · Perry §6

Conclusion & Call

Perry's conclusion crystallizes the proposition, addresses the FCF with Gospel resolution, and calls for a doxological response.

Perry — Conclusion Principles The conclusion should never introduce new material. It: (1) brings the sermon to a definite close, (2) re-establishes the proposition in a memorable way, (3) delivers the Gospel answer to the FCF, and (4) issues a specific call to response. Perry says: "Land the plane — don't circle the runway."
Peckham — Doxological Landing The conclusion is where relational doxology reaches its crescendo. The call should not be mere behavioral compliance but an invitation into deeper intimacy with God — where obedience itself becomes an act of praise in response to God's relational pursuit.
