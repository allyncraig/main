▶ Initial Sermon Prompt — Mark 2:1–12
Pericope: Mark 2:1–12 — The Healing of the Paralytic (ESV)

Central Question: What does this scene reveal about who Jesus is and what He came to do — and how does that truth address the paralysis of guilt and unbelief that every hearer carries?

Preaching Aim: Preach Christ as the Son of Man with full authority on earth to forgive sins and restore the whole person — body and soul — so that the congregation moves from paralysis to faith, from guilt to forgiveness, and from self-sufficiency to worship.

Key Tensions to Hold:
  • The crowd seeks access to Jesus — the scribes resist His authority. Which group does the congregation more resemble?
  • Jesus addresses the soul before the body. Why does the order matter — and what does it expose about what we seek from Him?
  • The four friends’ faith is active, persistent, and costly. What does that model for how the church brings the broken to Christ?
  • The scribes are theologically correct (only God forgives sins) but spiritually blind. How does correct doctrine miss the moment when Christ is standing in the room?

Redemptive-Historical Anchor: Jesus fulfills the Daniel 7:13–14 “Son of Man” who receives dominion and authority from the Ancient of Days. His word of forgiveness is grounded in His cross (Col 2:13–14). He is the great Physician who heals what sin has broken at the root.

Worshipful Goal: The sermon should end not in moral resolution but in doxology — “We never saw anything like this!” (v. 12). The congregation should leave with a fresh, astonished, grateful sense of the authority and tenderness of Jesus.

Observation

Rules 6, 8, 9, 10
What does the text say?
6. Contextually
Study the passage in its immediate setting. What comes before and after? What is the flow of thought?
8. Grammatically
Notice key words, repeated terms, commands, contrasts, and cause-and-effect statements.
9. Literally
Take words as they are intended. If the text uses a picture or figure of speech, explain the picture simply.
10. Literarily
Identify genre: narrative, poetry, prophecy, gospel, epistle, proverb, apocalyptic, or parable.

Interpretation

Rules 5, 7
What does the text mean?
5. Objectively
Ask what the author meant, not what we merely prefer the verse to mean.
7. Historically
Bring in setting, customs, geography, covenant moment, and people involved.

Correlation

Rules 2, 3, 4
What does Scripture say elsewhere?
2. Progressively
Trace how this truth unfolds across the Bible.
3. Harmoniously
Compare Scripture with Scripture so texts support rather than compete with each other.
4. Evangelically
Show how the text points to Christ, the gospel, and God’s saving purpose.

Application

Rule 1
How should I respond?
1. Reverently
Move from truth to worship, repentance, obedience, trust, love, and hope.

Biblical Prompts

Perry's Six Elements
Subject: the single topic the entire sermon is about — narrow enough to cover in one message.
Theme: adds a modifier to the subject. It shapes but does not yet assert — save the full claim for the proposition.
Proposition: a complete, present-tense, declarative sentence. Every main point must prove or develop it.
Interrogative: the question the proposition raises (e.g. How does Christ-centered devotion reorder life?). The answer supplies the main points.
Keyword: a plural noun that categorizes all main points together (e.g. "three responses that show…").
Transition: spoken aloud just before Point 1. Format: "There are [number] [keyword] that [interrogative adverb] [proposition]."
Self-Check
Is the proposition a full sentence with one clear subject and predicate?
Does the interrogative fit the proposition naturally?
Does the keyword gather the main points into one category?
Do the points rise from the passage rather than from imagination?
Can each point be explained, illustrated, and applied?

Textual Structural Diagramming Tool

Paste a passage with each clause or phrase on its own line, or paste a paragraph and let the tool break it into thought units. It will create a simple structural diagram in non-technical language, using indentation to show main ideas and supporting ideas.
Indentation logic: main actions stay farther left; supporting details move inward; contrast words like but, reason words like for/because, and purpose words like so that are tagged so you can see why lines were indented.
  • Left margin usually marks the main line of thought.
  • Indented lines usually show explanation, description, reason, result, or contrast.

TITLE GUIDELINES
✓ 2–5 words max — if it needs a comma, it’s too long
✓ Concrete image or tension — put the listener in a scene, not a doctrine
✓ Create a question — the best titles make the hearer ask “What does that mean?”
✓ Avoid jargon — no theological terms a non-churchgoer wouldn’t know
✓ Tension or paradox — e.g. “Holes in the Roof,” “The Friend Who Stays,” “Worth the Fall”
✓ Speak to the felt need — the title should whisper the introduction’s question
✓ Avoid alliteration in the title — save it for the points; titles need punch, not pattern
✓ Test it out loud — say it to a friend; if they lean in, you’re done
Examples for Mark 2:1–12: Holes in the Roof  ·  Just Say the Word  ·  The Mat You Carry  ·  Who Can Forgive?  ·  Four Friends and a Roof  ·  Rise
INTRODUCTION GUIDELINES
✓ Hook immediately — open with a story, question, or scene, not a commentary on what you’re about to say
✓ Surface the felt need — name the real-life struggle the text addresses before the passage is announced
✓ Earn the right to preach — make the listener feel understood before asking them to listen
✓ Move from life to text — start in the listener’s world, then invite them into the biblical world
✓ Create tension or a question — the introduction should raise a problem the sermon body resolves
✓ Keep it under 3 minutes — longer introductions drain energy before the text is even opened
✓ Avoid throat-clearing — no “good morning,” no retelling the bulletin, no “turn in your Bibles to…” yet
✓ End with the passage — land on the biblical text as the answer to the question you just raised
Structure: Hook (story/image) → Felt-need question → Bridge to text → Read the passage
CONCLUSION GUIDELINES
✓ Recap all main points — briefly restate each movement so the sermon lands as a whole
✓ End with Christ, not behavior — the final appeal is to trust and worship, not a to-do list
✓ One clear call to action — name the single most important response the text demands
✓ Return to the introduction’s image — closing the loop creates a satisfying, memorable ending
✓ Aim for doxology — the best conclusions leave the congregation in awe of God, not applauding the preacher
✓ Use the FCF — address the fallen condition one final time, then point to Christ as the only solution
✓ No new content — the conclusion is not the place to introduce a fourth point or a new idea
✓ End memorably — the last sentence should be short, weighty, and worth remembering all week
Structure: Recap points → Restate proposition → Gospel appeal (FCF → Christ) → Doxological close
